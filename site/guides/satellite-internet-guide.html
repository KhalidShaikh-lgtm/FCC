Satellite Internet: Is It Right for You?

Satellite internet reaches places that wired providers do not, which makes it a practical choice for many rural homes, cabins, and travel setups. It costs more than most wired plans for similar speeds and has trade-offs, so it makes the most sense when fiber, cable, DSL, and fixed wireless are not available at your address.

How satellite internet works

A small dish at your home sends and receives signals from a satellite in space. The satellite connects to a ground station that links to the wider internet. Because signals travel up and down, the connection has more delay than most wired service.

There are two main kinds of satellite service for homes:

  • Geostationary satellites. These orbit very high above the earth, about 22,000 miles up. Each one covers a wide area. Their distance means higher delay, known as latency.
  • Low-earth-orbit satellites. These orbit much closer to the earth, in large groups called constellations. Being closer lowers the delay, and the service often feels more like ordinary broadband.

Providers in this market have included Starlink, which uses low-earth-orbit satellites, and HughesNet and Viasat, which have used geostationary satellites. Offers, prices, and coverage change often, so check each provider's own site for current terms at your address.

When satellite makes sense

  • You live where wired internet is not offered, or only slow DSL is.
  • You are in a rural or remote area with limited options.
  • You need internet at a second home, a cabin, or an RV.
  • You need a backup connection for outages.

When to look at other options first

Several options may be available at your address before you consider satellite:

  • Fiber and cable. Usually the best speed and price where they are offered.
  • DSL. Slower, but often cheaper, and sometimes available in areas without cable.
  • Fixed wireless and 5G home internet. Uses cell towers to deliver service to your home. It can be a good choice when signal is strong.

What to check before you sign up

  1. A clear view of the sky. Trees, buildings, and hills can block the signal. Providers ask where the dish can go, and some check your address.
  2. Monthly price and equipment. Check the plan price, the dish and router cost, and whether you buy or rent the equipment.
  3. Data policy. Some plans include a set amount of high-speed data, then slow down. Others have no cap. Read the details, especially if you stream a lot.
  4. Contract length and fees. Ask about contracts, activation fees, and early termination charges.
  5. Speeds you can expect. Plans often list a range. Ask what is typical at your address.
  6. Latency. Ask whether the service uses low-earth-orbit or geostationary satellites. It makes a large difference to video calls and online games.
  7. Installation. Find out whether you install it yourself or a technician does, and what it costs.

What you can and cannot do on satellite

Works well for: web browsing, email, streaming video, remote work with occasional video calls, and general household use, especially on low-earth-orbit service.

Can struggle with: fast online games, because of delay; frequent large uploads; and very heavy use on plans with data limits.

If you work from home, test video calls during your trial or return window if one is offered. If you game, ask the provider about latency before you commit.

Weather and reliability

Heavy rain, thick snow, or storms can weaken the signal and slow or interrupt service. Snow on the dish can also block it. Most outages are brief, but if your work depends on a stable connection, consider a mobile hotspot as a backup.

Cost

Satellite plans often cost more per month than wired plans, and the equipment can be a one-time cost or a rental. Compare the total over the first year, including equipment and activation, and the monthly price afterward. A plan with a low introductory price can cost more once it ends.
