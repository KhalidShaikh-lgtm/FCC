How to Lower Your Internet Bill

Most households can cut their internet bill by checking three things: whether a promotional price has ended, what extras are on the bill, and whether a better plan is available at their exact address. This guide walks through each step in order, so you can finish in one sitting.

Step 1: Find out what you actually pay

Start with your last full bill, not the price you remember from sign-up. Add up every line: the internet plan, equipment rental, TV or phone bundles, taxes, and fees. The monthly total is the number to compare against any offer.

Promotional prices are the most common reason bills rise. Many plans start at a low rate for 12 to 24 months and then move to the regular rate, sometimes without a notice you remember reading. If your bill went up with no change in service, check the date you signed up and the length of the promotion.

Step 2: Look for charges you can remove

Small monthly items add up over a year. Check for these:

  • Equipment rental. Many providers charge a monthly fee for a modem or router. If your provider allows its own equipment, a compatible modem you buy once can end that fee. Ask which models work before you buy.
  • TV and phone add-ons. Bundled channel packages and landlines often stay on the bill long after they stop being used.
  • Premium extras. Security software, extra email accounts, and "whole home" Wi-Fi add-ons are often optional.
  • Data overage or unlimited-data fees. If your plan has a data cap, check whether you are paying for an unlimited option you do not need, or the reverse.
  • Paper bill or card fees. Some providers charge for paper statements or discount autopay.

If a charge is unclear, ask the provider to explain it in writing. A plain answer is reasonable to expect.

Step 3: Check you are not paying for more speed than you use

A faster plan costs more, and many homes do not use the extra speed. A household that streams video, works from home, and has several phones usually does well on a mid-range plan. Our guide on how much speed you need explains how to size a plan, and it is worth reading before you decide to downgrade or upgrade.

Before you change plans, run a speed test on a wired connection at a quiet time. If you pay for a high tier but the test is far below it, the problem may be your router or Wi-Fi coverage rather than the plan.

Step 4: Call your current provider first

Providers often have offers that are not advertised on the main plans page. A call or chat to the retention or loyalty team is a normal way to ask about them. These tips help:

  1. Have your account number and the monthly total in front of you.
  2. Say plainly that you are reviewing your bill and want to know the current options for your address.
  3. Ask for the new monthly total including all fees, and for how long that price is guaranteed.
  4. Ask whether the offer needs a contract or an early termination fee.
  5. Write down the date, the name of the person, and what they promised.

If the offer is good, ask for it in writing or by email before you agree. If it is not, you have lost nothing.

Step 5: Compare other providers at your address

Availability and prices vary by street, so compare providers that actually serve your address rather than the ones advertised in your area. Enter your ZIP code on YourBillCheck to see which providers report service nearby, then confirm your exact address with the provider before you switch.

When you compare, use the same yardstick for every offer:

  • The monthly price after the promotion ends, not just the first year.
  • Equipment and installation fees.
  • Contract length and any early termination fee.
  • Download and upload speeds.
  • Data caps, if any.

A lower headline price with a higher fee list is often not cheaper. Write each offer's real monthly total in a simple list and compare the totals.

Step 6: Watch for the traps

  • Promotional price cliffs. Put a reminder in your calendar about a month before a promotion ends.
  • Installation and activation fees. Ask whether they apply and whether they can be waived.
  • Bundles that cost more. A bundle is only a saving if you would buy every part of it anyway.
  • Switching without a plan to cancel. Cancel the old service only after the new service works, and return any rented equipment to avoid unreturned-equipment charges.

Step 7: Repeat once a year

The market changes, and so does your household. Check your bill once a year, or whenever a promotion ends. A ten-minute review often finds a saving.
