Best Internet Type for Streaming and Gaming

For streaming, you need enough download speed for the number of screens running at once. For gaming, a steady, low-delay connection matters more than a very high top speed. This guide explains what to look for in each case and how to fix the usual problems.

Streaming: what matters

Streaming video is mostly a download job. The video service sends a continuous flow of data to your device, and the quality you get depends on whether your connection can keep up.

  • Resolution. Standard and HD video use modest speeds. 4K video uses noticeably more, often in the range of 15 to 25 Mbps per stream, though exact figures vary by service.
  • Number of streams. Two people watching 4K at once need about twice as much as one. Add every stream that runs at the same time.
  • Other traffic. A phone update, a game download, or a video call during a movie can squeeze the stream.
  • Wi-Fi quality. A weak signal to the TV is a common cause of buffering, even on a fast plan.

A simple check: add the speeds of the streams you run together, then add some headroom for everything else in the house. If the total is well under your plan's download speed, speed is probably not your problem.

Gaming: what matters

Online games send small amounts of data back and forth very quickly. They use little bandwidth, so a modest plan can be enough. What decides how a game feels is different:

  • Latency (ping). The time it takes for data to travel to the game server and back, measured in milliseconds. Lower is better.
  • Jitter. How much that delay changes from moment to moment. A connection that is fast on average but jumpy feels bad in a game.
  • Packet loss. Data that never arrives, which can show up as stutter or rubber-banding.
  • Upload speed. Gaming itself needs little, but streaming your game to others, or uploading clips, uses more.

A very fast plan will not cure high latency. That depends on the type of connection, the distance to the game server, and your home network.

Fix the home network first

Many streaming and gaming problems come from the home network, not the plan. Try these steps before you pay for a faster tier:

  1. Use a cable for your console or PC. A wired connection avoids most Wi-Fi delay and drops. It is the single most useful gaming upgrade for most people.
  2. Move the router. Place it in a central, open spot, off the floor and away from thick walls and large metal objects.
  3. Use the 5 GHz band for nearby devices. It is usually faster at short range, while 2.4 GHz reaches farther.
  4. Update or replace an old router. Older models may struggle with modern speeds and many devices.
  5. Consider a mesh system for a large home. Several units cover more rooms evenly.
  6. Limit background downloads during play. Cloud backups and updates can raise your delay.

Data caps

Heavy streaming and large game downloads use a lot of data. Some plans have a monthly data cap, and going over can mean extra charges or slower speeds. Check your plan for a cap before you sign up. A household that streams a lot and downloads big games should look for a plan with no cap or a generous one.

How to pick a plan

  1. List what you do: number of streams, the games you play, and anything you upload.
  2. Use the sizing steps in our guide on how much speed you need to choose a tier.
  3. Prefer fiber or cable if they are available at your address.
  4. Compare the monthly total after any promotion, including equipment fees and data caps.
  5. Test your speed and latency at different times once you are connected.
